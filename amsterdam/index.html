Entry 01 · July 29, 2026 · status: preliminary

The morning the drains gave up

On the morning of July 29, 2026, severe storms dropped 3–9 inches of rain on the Capital Region and Mohawk Valley, at rates of one to two inches per hour. The National Weather Service issued Flash Flood Warnings for Montgomery County and the wider region.

Amsterdam's storm drains were overwhelmed. The Chuctanunda Creek — which falls steeply through the middle of the city — shed its water fast, and the Mohawk River gauge at Amsterdam showed rising stage. Route 5 (Main Street) and Route 30 closed, underpasses flooded, and the fire and police departments performed water rescues while DPW ran emergency pumping. The city and county advised against all non-essential travel.

Context · status: confirmed history

Why this keeps happening

Amsterdam floods in two different ways, and they need different fixes.

Heavy rain — like July 29

Urban storm drains are designed for ordinary storms, not extreme cells. When rainfall outruns the drains, streets flood from the surface down — no river required. This is the mechanism the existing floodwalls were never built to solve.

Ice jams — the winter mechanism

Ice blockages on the Mohawk back water up into the city suddenly. It has happened for as long as anyone has kept records — 1914, 1955, 1996.

The benchmark year

Hurricane Irene and Tropical Storm Lee, back to back in 2011, set the modern high-water marks and remain the comparison every serious flood conversation returns to.

What's already built

The Army Corps' Amsterdam Flood Damage Reduction Project — floodwalls, channel realignment, and pumping stations on the South Chuctanunda — addresses river flooding. Whether its components are all fully operational today is an open question below.

For residents · status: informational

If your home took water

My basement flooded. What do I do, in order?

  1. Document first — photos and video with timestamps, before any cleanup.
  2. Call your insurer and file the claim early. NFIP flood policies have a 60-day window.
  3. Report it to Amsterdam DPW — a reported flood becomes part of the city's damage record, which is what disaster declarations and grants are built on.
  4. Keep every receipt — cleanup, repairs, temporary housing.

I don't have flood insurance. Is there help?

  • NYS DEC Resilient Retrofits can fund home floodproofing up to $50,000 per household, with income-based priority.
  • If a federal disaster is declared for this event, FEMA Individual Assistance may cover serious damage.
  • Montgomery County Department of Social Services and the Red Cross handle immediate relief.

Am I in a flood zone?

Look up your address at msc.fema.gov. Zone AE means high risk — flood insurance is required with a federally backed mortgage. Zone X is lower risk — insurance optional, and worth considering anyway, because July 29 flooded plenty of Zone X streets from the surface down.

Where do I report road damage or blocked drains?

Amsterdam DPW or the City Alert Center — with the location, a photo, and the date and time. Road status lives at 511ny.org.

Has this been declared a disaster?

As of this writing, no federal or state disaster declaration covers this event. Declarations follow a formal damage-assessment process — which is exactly why documented, reported damage matters.

Income and vacancy figures carry real margins of error at this population size — read the comparison ratios as more solid than the exact dollar amounts.

The Greater Amsterdam School District's own enrollment records — a count, not a survey estimate — show 78% of students classified as economically disadvantaged and an 18% dropout rate against a state average of 5% (2024–25 year, most recent cohort). Rent burden and unemployment reading close to or better than state and national averages doesn't mean the hardship elsewhere on this table is exaggerated — a city can have both a genuinely low-cost housing stock and a genuinely struggling population at once, and the numbers say that's exactly the case here.

The plan · status: adopted, mostly unpublished

A new plan, five votes, and a city page that hasn't caught up

The city's Common Council adopted the 2026 Comprehensive Plan on May 19, 2026, by a vote of 5–0, alongside a formal environmental (SEQRA) negative declaration — confirmed directly from that meeting's Council minutes. It replaces a 2003 plan, which itself replaced a 1960 master plan; 23 years separate the last two.

As of this notebook's last check, the adopted plan has not been posted anywhere on the city's own website. The Comprehensive Plan Update page still reads "anticipated completion: Spring 2025" and links only the February 2026 draft — checked and unchanged as of today. If you go looking for it and can't find it, that isn't you missing something.

Three things worth asking about at a public meeting, because the public record doesn't currently answer them:

  • The February draft's own table of contents promised a "Roadmap for Action" — but its actual Section 4 is titled "Our Community Development Strategy," and the draft ends at page 84 with no action or implementation table. Council described the final pre-adoption edits as "a few grammar and picture updates" — does the adopted version contain the roadmap the plan told readers to expect, or not?
  • The new plan treats a 2023 update to the city's waterfront policy (the LWRP) as settled and current. The city's own website still labels that same 2023 document a "Draft." State records show only the original 1993 waterfront plan as formally approved. Which one actually governs a permit application on the waterfront today?
  • At a March 2026 Council meeting, a resident raised the planning process's lack of Spanish-language access; staff said the plan was "being translated." No Spanish-language version has been found posted since.

Don't confuse this with a separate, concurrent process next door: the Town of Amsterdam — a different government — is running its own zoning update on its own timeline, targeting spring 2027. The city and town are frequently conflated in casual searches and even in some press coverage; they are not the same body.

Recent track record

Amsterdam doesn't apply for this money from a standing start. In the last three years the city (or a partner applying on its behalf) has won a $2.5M state waterfront grant plus $150K from the Canal Corporation for the Southside Boardwalk (2023); $262,500 for a Veterans Park bath house (2025); and — the largest single win — roughly $21 million combined for a wastewater treatment plant upgrade: a $10M state water-quality grant, $4.3M more from a related infrastructure program, and a $7.04M no-interest state loan (2024, targeted completion mid-2027). The Amsterdam Industrial Development Agency separately won $296,475 for a rail-trail project in 2025, and Montgomery County won $30,000 to plan a Chuctanunda trail extension that runs through the city. This is the pattern any new flood-mitigation application would be judged against.

Right now

The 2026 round of the state's main grant application (the CFA) closed July 31; results aren't expected until fall. A related state water-infrastructure fund's 2026 application window closed a few days earlier, on July 27. No new round of the Downtown Revitalization Initiative or NY Forward has been announced yet — the last one (Round 9) went to Rome, Utica, and the Village of Morris in April 2026; Amsterdam was not an applicant that round.

Amsterdam starts with real advantages: existing flood-reduction infrastructure, an adopted Comprehensive Plan, an active pipeline of recent wins, and the 2011 flood record establishing precedent for the next one. The gating questions tend to be administrative rather than conceptual — a current hazard-mitigation plan, standing with the federal flood insurance program, an active federal registration — and they decide eligibility for nearly everything on this page.

DRI Round 3 · status: mixed, seven-plus years in

Ten million dollars, fifteen projects, one bottleneck

In 2018–2019 (see Discrepancies — even the award year isn't consistent across the city's own documents), Amsterdam won a $10 million state Downtown Revitalization Initiative grant, funding fifteen individual projects. The city's own DRI web page, as of this notebook's last check, publishes no per-project dollar amounts or status for any of them — everything below had to be pieced together from council records, state filings, and news coverage instead.

NYS Route 5 eastbound lane removalnot started$547,087 awarded; the city says it's "actively working with" the state transportation department, with no construction date. This is the one to watch — see below.

The Route 5 lane removal is the bottleneck for the rest of the list. It gates roughly $963,000 of other already-funded DRI work — the dog park, the skate park, recreation-center improvements, and progress on the transit-station site — and it alone has no construction date. The remaining projects among the original fifteen aren't itemized here because the city hasn't published their individual status; the honest tally as of this notebook's last check is two complete, one under construction, and the rest split between "in design" and "not started."

Outside the DRI list, four other sites define what "development" currently means in Amsterdam: the Chalmers Mills site (cleared over a decade ago; a 2023 housing deal lapsed; the Industrial Development Agency is remarketing it, now sitting beside the finished boardwalk and pedestrian bridge — a materially better pitch than it had in 2023); the former paper mill on Forest Avenue (a 2024 bakery-and-brewpub redevelopment deal fell through in 2026; the city is now seeking state permission to redirect its associated $1M award toward site preparation instead); the Lower Mills Complex on Elk Street (an October 2025 agreement handed foreclosure rights to a private developer who estimates $40M for mixed-use redevelopment; no timeline exists yet); and 24 Willow Street (a roof collapse forced emergency demolition; the Council formally declared it "slum and blight" in July 2026, a required step before the city can seek federal cleanup funding).

Elsewhere · status: for comparison, not prescription

Three cities, one hard decade, three different outcomes

None of these are Amsterdam, and none of them prove what will happen here. They're the closest available comparisons — similarly sized upstate cities running the same state programs through the same hard years — and one of them is an honest account of a plan that didn't survive contact with reality.

Gloversville — the closest playbook

Same region, a smaller and poorer tax base than Amsterdam's on paper, the same era of industrial collapse (gloves, not carpets). Won its own $10M DRI grant in December 2021; by mid-2025, four of its projects were complete, including turning a vacant lot into a concert and movie space, and a 75-unit mixed-income housing project was under construction leveraging roughly eighteen private dollars for every public one. Four and a half years in, and roughly halfway.

Cohoes — the reusable application

Almost exactly Amsterdam's size (18,159 people to Amsterdam's 18,166) and the best-argued application in this comparison. Its development corporation ran a small facade-grant program with its own money for six years before asking the state to scale it with $400,000 — proving the idea worked before asking anyone else to pay for it. Won $10M in March 2023; eleven projects were announced in 2024. It's too early for completions to judge yet.

Batavia — the honest failure

A $24.9M flagship — apartments, a restaurant, a brewery — broke ground in 2022. The developer went out of business in 2023, and the half-built site sat as an empty shell for roughly two years while a state agency and the developer's estate disputed what kind of building it even legally was. A nonprofit took it over in 2025; full occupancy is now targeted for 2027 — a decade after the original announcement. Batavia otherwise has the lowest vacancy rate of any city in this comparison. This wasn't a weak market failing; it was one company's collapse freezing the city's best block for two years with the city holding little leverage once construction stopped.

Discrepancies · status: flagged, not resolved

Six places the public record disagrees with itself

Building this notebook meant reading a lot of government documents against each other, and they don't always agree. Rather than quietly picking whichever number sounded more finished, here's where the record itself is inconsistent. Each one is a reason to ask a follow-up question, not an accusation.

① The award year

The city's own DRI web page says the $10M grant was awarded in 2018. The 2026 Comprehensive Plan and the city's Downtown Improvement Fund guidelines both say 2019. The state's announcement and the city's project pipeline appear to be running on two different calendars.

② How many people live here

Montgomery County's own Hazard Mitigation Plan gives two different 2020 populations for the city of Amsterdam — 31 people apart — in two different tables of the same document.

③ The median income

The newly adopted Comprehensive Plan cites $43,164, sourced to an older Census release. The current Census release says $56,178. Both numbers were correct when they were written — which is exactly why every figure on this page carries a date, including these.

④ One grant or two

A $700,000 2024 state award tied to a bakery redevelopment project appears twice in regional funding records, filed under two different program labels. Most likely one award counted twice rather than two separate awards — unconfirmed either way.

⑤ Is the clock tower funded

A 2021 regional funding ledger lists $500,000 for the restoration of the Sanford Clock Tower downtown. The state's own award database shows $0 for that same line item today — the pattern that usually means funding was pulled back, not that it was never real.

⑥ Which waterfront rules apply

The new Comprehensive Plan treats a 2023 update to the city's waterfront policy as settled and current. The city's own website still labels that same document a "Draft." State records show only the original 1993 plan as formally approved. Someone applying for a waterfront permit today deserves a straight answer about which one actually governs.

Open ledger · status: unknown, on purpose

What we don't know yet

Every cost of July 29 is UNKNOWN on this page until an official number exists — road repair, DPW and responder overtime, flooded basements, damaged vehicles, business interruption. We keep the ledger with empty columns rather than guesses, because a guessed number repeated twice becomes a fact.

The questions we're tracking, in rough order of consequence:

  • Does the city hold a current FEMA-approved Hazard Mitigation Plan? (It gates the biggest federal money.)
  • Is the city in good standing with NFIP, with an active federal SAM registration?
  • What were the peak gauge readings, and did the USACE pumping stations run at capacity?
  • How many water rescues, flooded homes, and damaged vehicles — the count that decides whether a disaster declaration is even requested?
  • Which streets that flooded on July 29 also flooded in 2011?
  • Will this event be reported into a state or federal declaration request?

When any of these gets an answer, the answer gets a source and a date here — and if we got something wrong, the correction stays visible rather than silently replacing the mistake.
